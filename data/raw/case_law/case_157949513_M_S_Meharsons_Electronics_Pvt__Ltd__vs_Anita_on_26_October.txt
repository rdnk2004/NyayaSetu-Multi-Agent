CASE_TITLE: M/S Meharsons Electronics Pvt. Ltd. vs Anita on 26 October, 2022
COURT: State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission
DATE: 2022-10-26
SOURCE_URL: https://indiankanoon.org/doc/157949513/
ATTRIBUTION: Powered by Indian Kanoon (https://indiankanoon.org)
REASONING:
The State Commission’s legal reasoning centers on the interpretation of "product liability" under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, specifically rejecting the appellant’s defense that a trader or dealer cannot be held liable for product defects in the absence of the manufacturer. The appellant argued that because they were merely a trader and not the manufacturer, and because the manufacturer was not impleaded as a party, no liability could be fastened upon them. However, the court countered this by invoking Sub-Section (34) of Section 2 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. The court emphasized that the statutory definition of "product liability" explicitly extends the responsibility to compensate for harm caused by defective products or associated deficiencies in service to both a "product manufacturer" and a "product seller." Consequently, the status of the appellant as a retail seller or trader did not absolve them of liability under the Act.

The court further established that the appellant was guilty of a clear "deficiency in service" due to their persistent inaction and failure to take corrective measures. The respondent had lodged numerous complaints regarding the malfunctioning of the air conditioners, yet the appellant failed to remove or rectify the defects. The court found that the appellant had installed the units despite the respondent pointing out physical damage to the inner coil and body of the air conditioners during the installation stage itself, under the false assurance that the units would function properly. By ignoring these visible defects and failing to resolve the subsequent cooling issues, the appellant actively deprived the consumer of the expected utility of a new product during the summer season. 

Ultimately, the Commission applied the statutory standards of service deficiency and product liability to hold the seller directly liable. The court concluded that the failure to redress the consumer's grievances over an extended period constituted a compensable harm, making the dealer liable for a refund and compensation. The Commission determined that the consumer did not need to prove the manufacturing defect through additional expert evidence or implead the manufacturer, as the seller's independent failure to address the delivery of physically damaged goods and subsequent non-performance fell squarely under the seller's product liability and deficiency in service as defined by the Act.
