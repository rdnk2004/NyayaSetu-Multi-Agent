CASE_TITLE: Ashok Gupta vs Indian Airlines Limited on 2 December, 2008
COURT: State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission
DATE: 2008-12-02

REASONING:
The court rejected a narrow, literal, and pedantic construction of Rule 17 of the First Schedule to the Carriage by Air Act, 1972, opting instead to apply principles of beneficial construction. The court reasoned that the reference to "bodily or personal injury" under Rule 17 and the terms of the carriage contract cannot be restricted to physical damage of the skin, bone, or tissues. Instead, "personal injury" must be interpreted in a wider sense as an invasion of personal rights, which encompasses mental injury, emotional distress, psychological trauma, physical discomfort, and psychological shock. Since the brain, mind, heart, and spinal cord are palpitating parts and the most vital organs of the human body, the severe psychological shock, physical discomfort of captivity, and the constant threat of death experienced by the passengers constitute actual, non-hypothetical bodily and personal injuries.

In establishing liability for deficiency in service, the court held that once a passenger enters into a contract with an airline, they place themselves at the disposal of the airline for a safe journey to their destination. Under Rule 17 and relevant security regulations, the internal security of the aircraft begins immediately at the stage of embarking, and the carrier is solely responsible for ensuring that no unauthorized persons, arms, or ammunition board the aircraft. The court determined that the airline's failure to conduct proper security checks at the embarkation point, which allowed hijackers to board the flight with weapons, constituted a severe lapse of duty and negligence, directly amounting to a "deficiency in service" under Section 2(1)(g) of the Consumer Protection Act, 1986.

Furthermore, the court clarified that the remedy under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 is independent of, and not in derogation of, any other law governing international or domestic carriers, as mandated by Section 3 of the Act. Consequently, consumers are entitled to compensation for mental agony, harassment, and emotional suffering arising from negligence, irrespective of any limited liability under carriage laws, with the limitation period being governed by Section 24-B of the Consumer Protection Act rather than carriage statutes. Finally, applying Section 14(1)(hb) of the Act, the court reasoned that when a large number of consumers who are not conveniently identifiable suffer the same injury, the consumer forum is empowered to treat each affected passenger on board as an individual complainant and award them uniform compensation to protect their collective interests.
