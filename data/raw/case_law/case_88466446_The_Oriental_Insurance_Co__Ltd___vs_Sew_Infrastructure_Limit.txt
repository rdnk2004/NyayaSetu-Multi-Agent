CASE_TITLE: The Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd., vs Sew Infrastructure Limited on 31 July, 2026

REASONING:
The State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission arrived at its decision by analyzing the statutory status of a licensed surveyor’s report under Section 64-UM(2) of the Insurance Act, 1938. Relying on settled precedents from the Supreme Court of India, including New India Assurance Company Ltd. v. Pradeep Kumar and National Insurance Company v. Hareshwar Enterprises (P) Ltd., the Commission emphasized that while a surveyor’s report is not so sacrosanct or conclusive that it can never be departed from, it is a document of statutory recognition and constitutes an essential, highly persuasive piece of evidence. The legal test applied dictates that a surveyor's report cannot be lightly brushed aside by an insurer unless there is more reliable, cogent, and contrary evidence on record to rebut its findings. 

Applying this test, the Commission held that the insurer’s repudiation of the claim was arbitrary, legally unsustainable, and amounted to a deficiency in service. Under the principles established in Sri Venkateswara Syndicate v. Oriental Insurance Company Ltd., although an insurer has the option to accept or reject a surveyor's report, any rejection must be based on acceptable, non-arbitrary grounds. In this case, the licensed surveyor conducted a meticulous, multi-visit assessment over two years, concluding that the damage was accidental, resulted from distinct hydrological occurrences, and did not fall under any policy exclusions. Because the insurer failed to produce any contrary technical, hydrological, or engineering expert evidence to discredit these findings, it could not reject the report based solely on its own subjective interpretation of the policy and the events.

Finally, the Commission affirmed the District Commission's findings concerning statutory compliance under the Consumer Protection Act. It ruled that the consumer complaint was filed well within the prescribed two-year statutory limitation period, as the cause of action legally accrued on the date the insurer formally repudiated the claim. Additionally, the Commission upheld the lower forum's exercise of pecuniary jurisdiction, which was correctly determined by looking at the premium paid as consideration. Consequently, the Commission concluded that the insurer's unilateral decision to ignore its own expert's quantum assessment without any documentary proof of a policy breach constituted an actionable deficiency in service, thereby justifying the award of the assessed claim amount with interest.
