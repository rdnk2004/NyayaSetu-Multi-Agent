CASE_TITLE: H.G. Jain vs Volkswagen India Pvt. Ltd. & 2 Ors. on 14 July, 2025
COURT: National Consumer Disputes Redressal
DATE: 2025-07-14

REASONING:
The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission clarified the scope of liability in a "Principal to Principal" relationship between a manufacturer and its authorized dealer under the Consumer Protection Act. The Commission held that even though the dealer and the manufacturer are independent legal entities where the dealer buys the cars for resale, the manufacturer cannot escape product liability or responsibility for manufacturing defects. This is because purchasers choose a vehicle based on the manufacturer’s advertised reputation, features, and specifications, and must necessarily interact with authorized dealers to purchase or service the vehicle during the warranty period. Therefore, the manufacturer remains directly liable to the consumer for any inherent manufacturing defects.

Regarding the statutory requirement of an expert opinion, the Commission interpreted Section 13(1)(c) of the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, ruling that obtaining a laboratory test or expert analysis is not mandatory in every dispute alleging a product defect. The statutory procedure is triggered only when the alleged defect cannot be determined without proper analysis or testing. The Commission established that where there is other "reliable, cogent and sufficient evidence on record"—such as extensive repair job cards, persistent breakdowns, and repetitive workshop visits shortly after purchase—the omission of an expert opinion is not fatal to the complainant’s case. The persistent necessity for major repairs itself serves as sufficient proof of an inherent defect.

Applying these legal standards, the Commission concluded that the vehicle suffered from active manufacturing defects and the opposite parties exhibited a deficiency in service. The persistent leakage from the sunroof, which damaged the vehicle’s electronic controls and repeatedly rendered it non-functional despite meager mileage and repair attempts, demonstrated a failure to resolve structural imperfections. The Commission emphasized that a consumer purchasing a high-end luxury vehicle is legally entitled to "reasonably good performance" rather than frequent workshop visits. Since the specific vehicle model was no longer in production, making replacement impossible, the Commission held the manufacturer and dealer jointly and severally liable to pay lump-sum damages for the harassment caused by the persistent and unresolved defects.
