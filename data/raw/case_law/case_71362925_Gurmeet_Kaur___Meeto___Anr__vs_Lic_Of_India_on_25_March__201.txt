REASONING:
The National Commission’s decision to dismiss the revision petition rests on the foundational legal principle of utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei) governing contracts of insurance. Applying the landmark precedent in Satwant Kaur Sandhu v. New India Assurance Company Ltd., the court emphasized that an insurance contract is a contract of speculation where the special facts determining the risk reside primarily within the knowledge of the assured. The court held that a "material fact" is any fact that would influence the mind of a prudent insurer in deciding whether to accept or reject the risk, or in determining the terms of the policy. Because there is a clear legal presumption that all information sought in the proposal form is material for the purpose of entering into the contract, the proposer is under a strict obligation to disclose those facts accurately. Consequently, any deliberate misstatement or withholding of such material information entitles the insurer to avoid the contract and repudiate its liability.

In applying this test to the facts, the court determined that the deceased life assured had committed a clear breach of his duty of disclosure. Under the early claim investigation required for policies where death occurs within two years of risk commencement, the insurer produced definitive medical evidence, including the certificate and oral testimony of the treating physician, Dr. R.K. Gupta. This evidence established that the deceased had been suffering from insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and chronic renal failure for several years prior to executing the proposal form, despite giving explicit negative answers to health-related questions in the questionnaire. Since the petitioners failed to successfully cross-examine the doctor or place any contrary evidence on record to rebut his findings, the court accepted the medical proof of pre-existing illness as indisputable, thereby validating the insurer's repudiation of the policy due to the suppression of material facts.

Finally, the court delineated the scope of its statutory powers under Section 21(b) of the Consumer Protection Act. Citing the Supreme Court's ruling in Mrs. Rubi (Chandra) Dutta v. M/s United India Insurance Co. Ltd., the Commission observed that its revisional jurisdiction is highly circumscribed and cannot be exercised to re-appreciate facts or substitute the lower forums' concurrent findings with a different factual interpretation. Revisional intervention is warranted only when there is a prima facie jurisdictional error, material irregularity, or a patent miscarriage of justice in the impugned order. Since the State Commission's order was based on sound appreciation of the evidence and established legal principles of insurance law, the court concluded that there was no jurisdictional or legal error to justify interference under Section 21(b).
